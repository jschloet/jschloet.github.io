1. How to play

Two players take turns placing coins on the board. Coins may not overlap and must stay inside the playing area.

If you cannot place another coin, you lose.

5. Why is this strategy so easy to compute?

In many two-player games, finding a guaranteed winning strategy means considering an enormous number of possible moves and future positions. Chess is an extreme example: the game tree is vastly too large to simply search exhaustively.

Here, we don't need to search. The geometry of the board gives us the algorithm.

On a centrally symmetric board with a playable centre, the first player can take the centre. After that, every opponent move has a matching move on the opposite side.

On a centrally symmetric board with no playable centre, the second player can do the pairing instead: every move has a distinct opposite partner.

A good algorithm doesn't always need to search. Sometimes, finding the right structure directly gives the answer.

Think about it

With two circles, Green could always respond in the other circle. What changes when there is a third?

Can you pair up moves in the outer circles and the middle circle?

Orange can guarantee a win!

Start by placing a coin at the centre of the middle circle. After each Green move, rotate its position 180° around the centre of the entire board:

  • In the middle circle, play directly opposite Green's coin.
  • In an outer circle, play at the matching position in the other outer circle.

The opening centre coin is its own mirror image; every other legal position has a different partner. Thus Orange can always respond to Green's move.

Exploit an opening mistake

Questions to investigate

Is every non-central opening a mistake that Green can exploit? Does the answer change with the coin size or how far from the centre Orange starts?

Try to give a guaranteed response strategy, not just one successful game.

The centre opening is a sufficient winning move for Orange, but that does not prove it is the only winning opening. If Orange opens off-centre, Green cannot simply invoke the usual 180° pairing: Orange's first coin is not at the fixed point, and its opposite position may interfere with later responses.

To claim Green can force a win, you would need a response to every possible Orange continuation. Experimenting may suggest candidate strategies, but a few victories do not prove one. This is why exploiting mistakes can be harder than following a known winning strategy.

A provably losing Orange opening

This works on the circle, square, and rectangle, provided there is enough room around the centre. Let each coin have radius r and call the board's centre O.

  1. Orange puts its first coin a distance 1.5r to the right of O.
  2. Green puts its coin a distance 1.5r to the left of O.
  3. After every subsequent Orange move, Green places the coin 180° opposite through O.

Why this wins: The two opening coins are mirror images, and together they block every location less than distance r from O. Thus any later legal coin centre is at least r from O, so its 180°-opposite partner is at least 2r away and cannot overlap it. The other two opening coins and the board boundary also respect the same rotation, so mirroring always preserves legality. Green responds to every Orange move; eventually Orange runs out of moves first.

More generally, this proof works for an opening distance r < d ≤ √3 r, as long as both initial coins fit and can be placed without overlap. For coin radius 18, a convenient example is d = 27 board pixels. The game uses a tiny extra clearance between coins, so positions right at the theoretical endpoints may not work; 1.5r is safely inside the range.

Try it yourself above: choose the shape, start Orange 1.5 coin radii right of the board's centre, and have Green place the mirrored coin. Then try to beat Green's mirroring strategy.
